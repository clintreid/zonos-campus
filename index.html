Tech Ridge · St. George, Utah

Zonos Campus

Zonos is exploring what its permanent headquarters should be. This site is the working record of that exploration: the ideas collected so far, the reasoning behind them, and the questions still open. Nothing here is a decision. It is how we are figuring out what we want the place to feel like.

Concept images throughout are illustrations used to test directions, not designs. Written in the first person by Clint Reid, founder and CEO.

The story so far

A global trade company in the desert

Zonos builds software for cross-border trade: customs, duties, taxes, and compliance. The mission is to create trust in global trade. It has its own section below.

The origin is specific. I spent about six years in international sales at UPS and DHL and kept meeting retailers who wanted to sell abroad. I concluded the barrier was not logistics, it was technology: nothing could calculate customs tariffs and taxes for e-commerce. I left shipping in 2009 and started the company, then called iGlobal, from an unfinished basement near Salt Lake City, doing the customer support and taking out the garbage myself. We still have the commercial invoice from our first international order. We moved to St. George in 2014, betting that world-class trade software could come from a desert town. We renamed the company Zonos on leap day 2018, at a live event held on this same ridge. We ran bootstrapped for twelve years, then raised a Series A led by Silversmith Capital Partners in September 2021, reported at the time as the largest in southern Utah history.

Today about 106 people work here, most of us in St. George, and revenue has tripled in the last 18 months without adding net headcount. I say the same thing to everyone, investors included: this is a generational company, not an exit. I plan to be here a long time. My version of it: sipping a Diet Coke at 80, still talking about the work we did last week.

In St. George the company has worked from unusual buildings on this mesa: first a purpose-built hangar on the old municipal airport, then a campus of 64 shipping containers beside it. Both were built quickly to hold growth. The hangar stays, kept as part of the story. The containers taught the team what an office should and should not be. This mesa was an airfield from 1929, a stop on the transcontinental airmail route, and the runway where SkyWest flew its first plane. The full history is on The runway page.

Now Zonos owns land on the east rim of the mesa and is planning its first permanent building: roughly 100,000 square feet, with a second building of similar size in a later phase. The move matters because the team expects to roughly double within a few years of moving in, and because the buildings Zonos occupies have always been part of how it tells its story to the people who fly here from around the world.

Why this matters

This is a generational building, not a real estate play. Zonos intends to occupy it for decades, which changes every decision: materials must age well, and the test for the design is daily use over decades.

The mission

Create trust in global trade

The mission has two halves, and both are meant literally. Zonos creates trust in global trade for its customers: a merchant, a carrier, or a postal operator who touches cross-border commerce should be able to trust every duty, tax, and declaration that moves through it. Zonos also holds itself to the same standard in how it operates as a business.

At its largest scale, the mission is about people, not shipments. A citizen in Japan downloads the Zonos app to ship to the United States and around the world. A grandmother sends a package to a grandkid who moved overseas, and it arrives without a surprise bill or a form she cannot read. Trade is how cultures reach each other, and most of what makes it hard is distrust encoded into paperwork. Each Zonos product removes a reason for people in two countries not to send something to each other.

Why this matters for the building

The countries on the meeting room doors, the globe at the entry, the cipher you can walk into, the customer flown in from another continent who feels expected: a headquarters for connecting cultures should itself feel like the world, gathered in one place in the desert.

How we want to do this

Collaborative, not prescriptive

I want to tell our story, and then I want a partner to help us figure the building out. Everything on this site describes how the place should feel and how the team actually works; the answers should come from the design partnership. I am not locked into ideas, and I say clearly when I am.

  • Feel over look. The pages here describe reactions and ways of working. The form that produces them is open.
  • The idea list is long because we are still collecting. Editing comes later, with the design team.
  • The team gets observed, not surveyed. The design should come from watching how people here actually work, without me in the room.
